News vs. Information vs. Research

Nice Distinctions

Same Scientific Content, Three Unique Formats!

For your current events assignment, you need a NEWS ARTICLE, not an informational website.

This guide will help you distinguish between news reporting, informational websites, and scholarly research.

News Article

What it is: Reporting on recent events written by journalists for the general public

Look for:
  • Reporter’s byline (author name)
  • Current date (within days/weeks of today)
  • News publication name (NYT, BBC, local paper, etc.)
  • Quotes from experts or witnesses
  • Information about a recent event

Informational Website

What it is: Background information, guide, fact sheet, or advocacy content

Look for:
  • No author name
  • Undated (or outdated)
  • Lack of publication name
  • No quotes
  • General background info
  • Reference works belong here, too. An encyclopedia entry may contain an author, a date, and a publisher, but it still qualifies as background information. The source is intended as an introduction to basic information, not analysis or particulars about an event.

Example Encyclopaedia Britannica (opens in a new tab)

Scholarly Article

What it is: Peer-reviewed research published in academic journals

Look for:
  • Multiple authors with institutional affiliations
  • Journal name
  • Content organized into sections such as abstract, methods, references
  • Technical language
  • Research findings or data

Beware!

It probably isn’t news if…

  • …the article is undated or outdated (older than a few months).
  • …there’s no byline or attribution (no author).
  • …most of the writing provides background information (no recent event).
  • …the content is labeled as a guide, fact sheet, or brochure (that’s an informational website).
  • …the format includes sections like an abstract, methods section, or long bibliography (that’s a scholarly article).
  • …the content is a reference work such as an encyclopedia or handbook, even if it’s signed and dated. Background information is just that: information.

          Thompson’s Three Cs for Source Evaluation

          Anytime you use a source, you should evaluate it. You evaluate a source by asking who created it and why; gauging how accurate and current it is; and considering where and how you found it.

          I encourage my students to use the Three Cs outlined below to ensure their research is reliable, relevant, and strong. Questions like these help you avoid misinformation, support better academic work, and build a foundation for wise decision-making inside and outside of school.

          Use this framework to quickly evaluate a potential source. Let the questions below guide you, but don’t answer all of them!

          Creator

          Who created this source? Is an author or authorial group identified? What are the creator’s academic and/or professional credentials? Are they a recognized expert in their field? Who or what makes them an authority? Is the creator affiliated with institutions or organizations? What other connections are revealed by a cursory search?

          Content

          Does the content provide ample attributions and citations? Are its sources easy to locate and verify? How does this information square with your prior knowledge about and other sources on the subject? Is it reasonable? How is the material written and organized? Whom is it addressing? What questions does it strive to answer? What positions does it take? When was the content last updated? When was it first created?

          Context

          Where, why, how, and by whom was this content disseminated? Who funded its creation? Where and how did you find it? What other materials exist alongside it? Can you locate reviews of the content or its creator? Why does it exist? Whose agenda(s) does it serve?

          A Note on Churnalism

          What is churnalism?

          Churnalism describes a variety of newswriting in which journalists, staffers, or untrained amateurs repackage third-party materials, such as press releases and wire statements, as news articles.

          It’s also referred to as “copy-and-paste journalism.”

          Churnalism is especially common in scientific reporting.

          Why is that a problem?

          These articles…

          • …are often identical to or lightly edited versions of promotional materials.
          • …do not analyze, interpret, or critically engage with the information they present.
          • …have undergone little to no fact-checking.
          • …tend to lack citations and attributions.
          • …do not incorporate additional research, such as the opinions of other experts or information about current scientific consensus on a subject.

          Why does churnalism abound?

          Good question! Reasons vary:

          • Many news outlets shuttered their science sections years ago. Although they don’t employ science writers, they still want to publish scientific news.
          • Organizations with declining revenue are desperate for clicks and content. Provocative headlines offer a slick way to spur engagement.
          • In the United States, public relations staff outnumber journalists. Overworked journalists don’t have the time or resources to generate original news stories. It’s much easier to put a twist on something somebody else already wrote.
          • Individual persons or small groups of people with a passion for a given subject can and do create websites, social media accounts, newsletters, and other forms of media.
          • “Entrepreneurs” attempt to generate revenue through automated processes, search engine optimization, and social media engagement. “Scientific” content making bold claims beneath flashy headlines farms engagement.

          Is churnalism necessarily a bad thing?

          It doesn’t have to be! Churnalist articles can be informative and illuminating. That said, it’s bad practice to uncritically accept any information, much less scientific knowledge.

          How can I identify churnalism?

          Tell-tale signs of churnalism include:

          • Headlines clearly intended to trigger an emotional reaction (fear, anger, sadness, etc.)
          • Lack of a byline (no author or date at the top of an article)
          • No context or single-source stories (all the information comes from one study or survey)
          • Extrapolation (article uses a single piece of information to make a larger claim about what’s likely or possible)
          • Few citations or attributions
          • One-sided perspective (e.g. only speaking with the authors of a study)
          • Excessive quotations (large portions of the article are in quotes)

          Is it more common in certain places?

          Often, yes! Sites like ScienceDaily and ScienceBlog are almost entirely churnalism. ScienceBlog, by its own account, now uses AI tools in research, drafting, and editing. That said, plenty of traditional media sources dabble in churnalism, too.

          Any tips for a quick check?

          Absolutely! You can:

          • Copy and paste a phrase, sentence, or paragraph from the article into a search engine to see how many versions of it exist elsewhere
          • Spot obvious tells like “according to a press release” or “a spokesperson said”
          • Clock PR jargon (words like “revolutionary,” “groundbreaking,” “world-class,” etc.)
          • Keep an eye out for ambiguous phrases such as “according to research,” “research published today,” and “according to a new report”
          • Look for phrases or headlines intended to spark an emotional reaction (e.g. “increase/decrease risk of,” “link between,” etc.)

          Writing About Science

          In the sciences, researchers make claims while journalists report them. If you’re writing about a study, every assertion you make should be bound by that study’s actual limits.

          It’s good practice to identify both the study’s finding and its specific limits early in your interpretation of the work.

          The cat study, for instance, measured greeting vocalizations in the first 100 seconds after a human walked through the door. “Cats meow more at men” is at once an oversimplification and, potentially, an exaggeration.

          Always attribute the claim in the body of your writing.

          Try to identify the researchers, the journal, and the publication year in the same sentence as the claim. Don’t say "a study found" or even "this study found"; rather, specify "A 2025 study published in Ethology by a Turkish research team reported that cats vocalized more frequently when greeting male caregivers."

          Tell your reader what, specifically, the study claims.

          "During the first 100 seconds after a caregiver walked through the door, the 31 cats averaged 4.3 sounds greeting men and 1.8 greeting women (average figures derived from study data)." The meowing is meaningless unless all three data points—the length of the measurement period, the sample size of cats, and the average number of vocalizations for each caregiver gender—are included. In my example, the averages are calculated from the study’s values. If you do any arithmetic to parse data for your reader, be sure to say so!

          If the researchers hedge their findings, reiterate that hedge!

          Look for words and phrases like "suggests," "implies," "is associated with," "may reveal," "could contribute to," "provides a foundation," and so on. Researchers rarely use absolutes in their writing, so regard "proves" and "causes" with caution. Do not attach a certain word to an uncertain claim!

          In the sciences, paraphrases overwhelmingly outnumber direct quotations.

          Only use verbatim quotations when the exact wording is important; otherwise, paraphrase findings directly from the study. If you’re not sure how to summarize what you’ve read, you don’t understand it yet! Do the cognitive work before you attempt to explain the study to an audience.

          Examine the study for flaws, shortcomings, and suggested improvements.

          Does it rely on a small sample size? Is one of the variables too unwieldy? Could the methodology stand a few tweaks? Identifying issues that potentially influenced a study’s outcome allows future researchers to iterate and expand on its findings.

          If I read your work, then read the study, I shouldn’t notice any disagreements! Your goal as a writer is accurately representing what the researchers found.
